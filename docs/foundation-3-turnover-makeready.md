# Foundation Guide 3
# Turnover & Make-Ready in Property Management

**A primer for someone with no real estate background**
Theory only — no automation, no technical content.

---

## Why This One Is Different

The other three problems are about information not moving. This one is about **money leaking in real time**.

Every day a unit sits empty is rent that will never be recovered. Not delayed — gone. You can't sell yesterday's vacancy later. That makes turnover the one problem in property management where the cost is continuous, measurable, and obvious to everyone involved.

It's also the problem where the property manager and the owner want exactly the same thing, which makes it unusually easy to talk about.

---

# PART 1 — Industry Foundations

## Vacancy is the industry's defining cost

A rental property has one job: produce rent. When it's empty, it produces nothing while continuing to cost money — mortgage, insurance, taxes, utilities, landscaping, HOA fees.

**And the property manager's revenue disappears at the same moment.** Remember that the management fee is a percentage of rent *collected*, typically 8–12%.[^1] An empty unit pays the manager nothing.

This is worth pausing on because it changes how you pitch. In most of your other products you're asking a manager to spend money to reduce a risk or save time. Here you're asking them to spend money to protect **their own revenue**, not just their client's. Their interests and the owner's are perfectly aligned.

## The vocabulary of vacancy

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Vacancy** | The unit is empty |
| **Vacancy loss** | Rent not collected because a unit was empty |
| **Days vacant** | Days from move-out to the new tenant moving in |
| **Days on market (DOM)** | Days from listing to lease signing |
| **Turn / turnover** | The complete cycle of one tenant leaving and another arriving |
| **Make-ready** | The physical work of preparing a vacated unit for the next tenant |
| **Rent-ready** | The state where a unit can be shown and leased |
| **Turn time** | Days from move-out to rent-ready |
| **Lease-up** | The marketing and leasing process |

**Note the distinction between turn time and days vacant.** Turn time is the physical preparation. Days vacant includes the leasing period afterwards. Make-ready coordination affects turn time directly, and days vacant indirectly — because you can't market a unit properly until it's presentable.

## The numbers that define the problem

From NAA's 2024 Income/Expense IQ benchmarks, published August 2025 — a neutral trade association source:[^2]

- **Turnover costs up 17.5% year over year**
- **Vacancy and rent loss at $1,323 per unit** — the fourth consecutive annual increase
- **Repairs and maintenance up 28.2% cumulatively since 2021**

Other benchmarks:
- Total turnover cost, all-in: **$3,872–$4,000 per unit**
- Make-ready cost alone: **$1,000–5,000 per unit**, typically taking 5–10 days[^3]
- Best practice turn time: **3–7 days**
- Actual average: **9+ days**, with some running 2–3 weeks[^4]

**The gap between 3–5 days and 9+ days is the entire opportunity**, and it's worth doing this arithmetic once so it's instinctive:

> A unit renting at $1,500/month costs about $50/day empty. Cutting four days off a turn saves $200. A 300-door portfolio at 40% annual turnover runs about 120 turns a year. Four days each is **$24,000 a year.**

# PART 2 — The Turnover Lifecycle

## The full sequence

Turnover begins earlier than newcomers expect. It starts at **notice**, not at move-out.

### Phase 1 — Notice period (before move-out)

| Step | Detail |
|---|---|
| **Notice to vacate received** | Tenant gives written notice, typically 30–60 days |
| **Log the notice date** | Calculate the target rent-ready date immediately[^5] |
| **Pre-move-out inspection** | Scheduled 7–14 days before move-out, ideally with the resident present[^5] |
| **Early scope assessment** | What's likely needed. Prevents surprises on move-out day |
| **Pre-marketing** | Some managers list the unit before it's vacant |

The pre-move-out inspection matters more than it sounds. It's the manager's chance to tell the tenant what will be charged against their deposit — which reduces disputes — and to line up vendors before the clock starts.

### Phase 2 — Move-out and assessment

| Step | Detail |
|---|---|
| **Keys returned** | **The vacancy clock starts here.** Every subsequent hour costs money |
| **Move-out inspection** | Should happen within 24 hours[^6] |
| **Punch list created** | The specific scope of work for this unit |
| **Deposit assessment** | Which items are tenant damage vs normal wear |
| **Owner approval** | For anything above the maintenance limit |

A bad punch list at this stage compounds at every downstream step.[^6] If the inspector misses something, it's discovered mid-turn — by which point vendors are scheduled, sequence is set, and adding work means starting parts of it over.

### Phase 3 — The work

This is where sequencing matters, and it's the heart of the problem.

| # | Stage | Typical duration | Trade |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Trash-out / clearing | 1 day | Junk removal |
| 2 | Repairs and trades | 1–3 days | Plumber, electrician, handyman, drywall |
| 3 | Paint | 1–2 days | Painter |
| 4 | Flooring | 1–2 days | Flooring installer |
| 5 | Deep clean | 1 day | Cleaner |
| 6 | QA walkthrough + photos | 0.5 day | Manager or inspector |

### Phase 4 — Back to market

Listing, syndication to portals, showings, applications, screening, lease signing, move-in.

## Why the sequence is fixed

This is the concept a newcomer most needs to internalise:

> *Done right, it sequences as: repairs and trades first, then paint, then flooring, then final deep clean and QA walkthrough. **Reverse any of those and you'll re-clean, re-paint, or re-walk the unit at least once.***[^6]

Work through why:

- **Repairs before paint** — drywall patching creates dust and needs painting over. Paint first and you paint twice
- **Paint before flooring** — paint drips and overspray. New flooring under a paint job gets damaged or masked at extra cost
- **Cleaning last** — every trade before it creates dust and debris. Clean early and you clean twice
- **QA after cleaning** — inspecting a dirty unit tells you nothing about its finished state

**The failure isn't just delay — it's rework.** A resequenced turn doesn't take longer because people work slower; it takes longer because work is done twice. That's an important distinction to be able to explain, because it means the cost isn't only vacancy days, it's duplicated labour and materials too.

# PART 3 — The Problem

## Where the time actually goes

Here's the finding that defines this problem:

> *A standard apartment make-ready takes 5–10 days from move-out to rent-ready; operators who sequence tightly hold it to 5–7. **Most of the calendar beyond that is coordination lag between trades, not hands-on work.***[^6]

Read that twice. **The work isn't slow. The gaps between the work are slow.**

The painter finishes Tuesday at 2pm. The manager doesn't find out until Thursday morning. The cleaner is called Thursday afternoon and can't come until Monday. That's six days of nothing happening on a unit that was ready for its next step on Tuesday.

A mid-sized Texas operator described losing 2–3 days just waiting for vendors to confirm or show up, with phone tag between tenants and contractors consuming 10–15 hours a week per portfolio.[^7]

## Why the coordination breaks — five reasons

**1. Vendors are independent businesses with their own schedules.**
The painter isn't an employee. He has four other clients. He finishes and moves to his next job — telling the manager isn't on his list.

**2. There's no completion signal.**
The manager finds out a stage is done by calling and asking. That means proactively remembering to ask, about the right unit, at the right time, across every unit in turn.

**3. A stalled unit looks identical to a working unit.**
Both look like nothing in an inbox. There's no view showing "unit 12 has been sitting at paint for three days."

**4. The coordinator is holding it all in their head.**
Fifteen units in turn, each at a different stage, each with a different vendor. Human working memory is not built for that, and the failures are silent.

**5. Trash-out is a chronic bottleneck.**
Junk removal is repeatedly identified as the most common single stage delaying the entire timeline.[^3] It's the first stage, so everything downstream inherits its delay.

## The deposit dimension

Turnover carries a second, parallel workflow that generates real friction: **the security deposit settlement.**

The manager must determine which damage is **normal wear and tear** (landlord's cost) and which is **tenant damage** (deductible from the deposit).[^8] Complications:

- **Age matters.** HUD life-expectancy guidelines are used to prorate. Carpet at the end of its useful life can't be charged at full replacement cost[^8]
- **Documentation is essential.** Move-in photos and condition reports are the evidence base
- **Deadlines are statutory.** California, for example, requires an itemised statement within 21 days of move-out[^9]
- **Disputes are common.** Security deposit deductions are among the leading causes of landlord-tenant conflict[^9]

You're unlikely to build for this directly, but you need to know it exists — because it's happening in parallel and competing for the same person's attention during the same window.

## Who's involved

| Role | What they do |
|---|---|
| **Property manager / coordinator** | Owns the timeline, coordinates everyone |
| **Inspector** | Move-out inspection, punch list, final QA |
| **Trash-out crew** | Clears left-behind items |
| **Handyman / trades** | Repairs — plumbing, electrical, drywall, hardware |
| **Painter** | Patching and painting |
| **Flooring installer** | Carpet or hard flooring |
| **Cleaner** | Deep clean |
| **Leasing agent** | Marketing, showings, application processing |
| **Owner** | Approves spend above the maintenance limit |
| **Bookkeeper** | Deposit settlement, invoices, owner billing |

Nine to ten parties on a single turn. **Every boundary between them is a place where days disappear.**

# PART 4 — What It Costs Them

**Direct vacancy loss.** The obvious one. $50–66/day per unit, compounding across every turn, every year, and unrecoverable.

**Rework.** Resequenced work means double cleans, repainting, re-inspections. Real money that never appears as a line item — it's absorbed into "the turn took 11 days."

**Owner confidence.** Owners see days vacant on their statement. A manager consistently turning units in 12 days when the market standard is 5 has a conversation coming, and eventually loses the door.

**Lost leasing season.** Rental markets are seasonal. A unit that misses the summer window may sit substantially longer or lease at a lower rent.

**Staff time.** 10–15 hours a week per portfolio spent on phone tag[^7] is a meaningful share of a coordinator's capacity, spent on work that produces nothing.

**A rising baseline.** Turnover costs rose 17.5% in a single year, and vacancy loss has risen four years running.[^2] The problem is getting more expensive, not less — which means the value of solving it rises annually.

## What good looks like

One operator reduced average turnover from **14 days to 5 days** in the Phoenix market specifically by eliminating sequencing bottlenecks and coordinating stages in the correct order, rather than waiting on multiple vendors whose schedules created cascading delays.[^10]

Nine days per turn. At $50/day across 120 turns, that's $54,000 a year — from sequencing, not from working harder.

# PART 5 — What You Are Actually Solving

## Name the job precisely

You are **not** solving:
- How fast vendors work
- Vendor availability or capacity
- The cost of materials
- Whether the scope was assessed correctly
- Owner approval delays

You **are** solving:
- Nobody knows a stage finished until they ask
- There's no view of which units are stalled and for how long
- The next vendor isn't notified promptly
- Stages get done out of order, causing rework
- Coordination lives in one person's head

**In one sentence:** *you are closing the gap between "a stage finished" and "the next stage started."*

## The one thing that determines whether this works

**Will the client's vendors report completion?**

Everything depends on it. If the painter texts when he's done, the whole thing works. If he doesn't, you're automating a signal that doesn't exist.

This isn't a technical question — it's a fact about a specific client's vendor relationships, and it varies enormously between companies. Establish it before anything else.

**The question to ask:** *"When your painter finishes a unit, how do you find out?"*

## An important secondary benefit

Even where vendor reporting is patchy, **visibility alone has value**. A board showing every unit in turn, its stage, and days elapsed changes how a coordinator works — because a stall becomes visible instead of invisible.

The system doesn't need vendors to be reliable. It needs to detect when they aren't.

## Benefits, in the order a prospect cares

1. **Fewer vacant days**, which converts directly to recovered rent
2. **Less rework** from out-of-sequence stages
3. **Stalls become visible** instead of being discovered late
4. **The coordinator stops making status-check calls**
5. **Better owner reporting** — days vacant with an explanation attached
6. **A repeatable process** rather than one person's memory

# PART 6 — Talking to Prospects

## The gating question — ask this first

> **"When your painter finishes a unit, how do you find out?"**

| Answer | Meaning |
|---|---|
| "He texts me" / "He sends a photo" | A signal exists. Strong prospect |
| "I call him" / "I drive by" | No signal to automate. Weak prospect |
| "He marks it in the portal" | Probe — is that real or aspirational? |

## Other questions worth asking

- *"What's your average days from move-out to relisted?"* — anchors all the arithmetic
- *"How many units are in turn right now, and how long has each been sitting?"* — if they can't answer instantly, that's the pitch
- *"What's a typical monthly rent across your portfolio?"* — you need this for the ROI conversation
- *"Do you ever end up cleaning or painting twice?"* — surfaces the rework cost, which they often haven't quantified
- *"How many turns do you do in a year?"*

## The arithmetic close

This is the only one of the four problems where you can compute a real annual number live, on their figures, during the call. Use it.

> *"You said eleven days average and about $1,600 typical rent — that's roughly $53 a day. Take three days off each turn across ninety turns a year and that's about $14,000."*

Let them do the last step of the arithmetic themselves. It's more convincing when it's their conclusion.

## What to listen for

| They say | It means |
|---|---|
| "About two weeks, usually" | Real gap against the 5–7 day benchmark |
| "It depends on the vendors" | They know coordination is the bottleneck |
| "We had one sit for a month" | An existing wound |
| "We use a turnkey service" | May be outsourced already. Probe |
| "My maintenance guys do the whole turn in-house" | No handoffs to instrument. Weak prospect |

## What NOT to say

| ❌ Don't | ✅ Do |
|---|---|
| "This will make your vendors faster" | "This closes the gap between one finishing and the next starting" |
| "This automates your turnover" | "This tracks the handoffs so nothing sits waiting" |
| "Your vendors will use the system" | "Your vendors reply to a text, same as now" |

## Misconceptions a newcomer will have

**"The delay is because the work takes a long time."**
It isn't. Most of the excess calendar is coordination lag between trades, not hands-on work.[^6]

**"Vendors will adopt a portal or app."**
They won't. Contractors do not install apps, create accounts, or learn portals for one of their many clients. Anything requiring more than replying to a text will fail.

**"The manager knows what's happening with their units."**
They know about the three they thought about this morning. Ask about a specific unit and you'll often get a pause.

**"Turnover starts at move-out."**
It starts at notice. There's typically 30–60 days of preparation time that most companies underuse.

**"Faster is always better."**
A rushed turn that produces a unit failing QA costs more than a well-sequenced one. The goal is eliminating dead time, not compressing the work.

## The Six Things to Remember

1. **Vacancy is unrecoverable.** Delayed rent can be collected later; lost rent cannot.
2. **The manager loses money too**, because their fee is a percentage of rent collected. Interests are aligned.
3. **The work isn't slow — the gaps are.** Most excess calendar time is coordination lag between trades.
4. **Sequence errors cause rework, not just delay.** Cleaning before repairs means cleaning twice.
5. **Turnover starts at notice**, not at move-out. There are 30–60 days of preparation time most companies waste.
6. **Everything depends on whether vendors report completion.** Establish that before anything else.
