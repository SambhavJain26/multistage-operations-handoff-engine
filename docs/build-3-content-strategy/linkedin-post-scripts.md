### The post copy that sits above the document

> Half of unit turns take nine days or more. The benchmark is three to seven.
>
> The gap is not slow trades. A standard make-ready contains a few days of actual work, and most of the rest of the calendar is coordination lag between one trade and the next.
>
> Here is what that looks like on a real unit. A painter finishes Tuesday afternoon. Nobody finds out until Thursday, because he mentioned it to a colleague rather than to anyone tracking the unit. >
> Nine slides, and a way to count your own.

## Post 3. A vendor who finished and said nothing looks exactly like one who never started

**Format:** document carousel, 8 slides. **Framework:** Problem-Proof. **Pillar:** silence as the signal. **CTA:** none.

**Document title (50-char limit):** `Silence looks identical either way` (34 characters)

**On the framework.** Problem-Proof wants a specific claim on slide 1 and a receipt at the end. The claim is the two-mornings problem, and the receipt is the alert design: what the system does with silence, including the three restraints that took longer to decide than the rule itself.

### The post copy that sits above the document

> One unit. Two mornings, a week apart. Both times I opened the inbox first thing and found nothing from the painter. Same empty thread, same unit sitting there, same total absence of news.
>
> On the first morning he had not started.
>
> On the second, he had finished two days earlier and never mentioned it to anyone.
>
> Same inbox. Same silence. Opposite meanings, and nothing anywhere to tell them apart.
>
> Every system a property manager uses is built to record what happened. A message arrives, a status changes, someone ticks a box. Nothing is built to notice the thing that did not happen.
>
> Which leaves silence as the most common state in a turn and the only one with no entry anywhere. It costs the same per day as any other delay, roughly monthly rent divided by thirty, and it is the one condition nobody is watching.
>
> The fix is not complicated. Give every stage an expected duration, then treat passing that duration with no word as information rather than as nothing.
>
> The hard part was restraint, and it took longer to settle than the rule did.
>
> It fires once per stage, because an alert that repeats gets muted and a muted alert is the same as no alert. It holds outside business hours, because a stall at 2am is still a stall at 8am and nobody was going to call a flooring crew at 2am anyway. And it can be snoozed, because if you already know why a unit is stuck, being asked again is just noise.
>
> Eight slides on treating an absence as an event.

*Word count: 266. Characters: 1446. Em dashes: 0. First 210 characters end inside "On the first morning h", so the second morning, which is the whole point, is withheld past the fold. Verified by measuring the cut, not by estimating it.*

**Slide 2 — Reframe the problem**

**Slide 7 — The detail**
> A snooze that means something.
> If you know why it is stuck, say so.
> A system you cannot quiet is one you switch off.

**Slide 8 — Close**

> What happened is recorded somewhere already.
> What did not happen is where the days go.

## Post 5. Seven ordering rules for a unit turn, and what each one costs

**Format:** document carousel, 10 slides. **Framework:** Value-Stack. **Pillar:** turn literacy. **CTA:** none.

**Document title (50-char limit):** `Seven ordering rules for a unit turn` (36 characters)

**This is the reference post.** It should be the one a coordinator screenshots. The cover promises exactly seven and exactly seven are delivered, per the Value-Stack rule about counts being checkable promises.

### The post copy that sits above the document

> The most common sequencing failure in a unit turn is flooring going in before the paint is finished.
>
> It is almost never ignorance. It is material arriving late, a crew with a free slot tomorrow, and somebody trying to claw back two days. Everyone involved knows the right order. The order loses to the schedule.
>
> What it costs is floor protection, drip risk, and occasionally replacing flooring that went down the week before.
>
> That is one rule. There are seven, and each one exists because somebody paid for the lesson.
>
> Clean before the repairs are done and you buy a second clean. Scope repairs before the move-out inspection and you are scoping from memory, then finding the rest on the second visit. Market a unit before anyone has walked it and you apologize to a prospect standing in it.
>
> Across the industry, twenty to twenty-five percent of turnover repairs already need a second visit. Sequencing is not the only reason for that number, but it is the reason you can actually remove.
>
> The thing I did not expect is that writing the order down beats knowing it. Everyone knows repairs come before paint. Nobody is thinking about that at four on a Friday when the painter has availability and the drywall patch is "basically done."
>
> A rule on its own gets overridden by whoever is standing in the unit. A rule with its reason attached usually survives the conversation.
>
> Ten slides: the seven rules, and what each one costs when it breaks.

*Word count: 252. Characters: 1439. Em dashes: 0. First 210 characters end inside "It is material arriving late", so the cause is named and the cost is withheld past the fold.*

### Slides

**Slide 1 — Cover**
> Seven ordering rules for a unit turn.
> And what each one costs when you break it.

**Slide 2 — The problem**
> These are not knowledge failures.
> Everyone knows repairs come before paint.
> Nobody is thinking about it at 4pm on a Friday.

**Slide 3 — Rule 1**
> Trash-out waits for move-out.
> Break it and the crew cannot clear the unit.
> You pay for the visit anyway.

**Slide 4 — Rule 2**
> Repairs wait for move-out.
> You cannot scope what you cannot see.
> Break it and you find the rest on visit two.

**Slide 5 — Rule 3**
> Paint waits for repairs.
> Patch after painting and you paint that wall twice.
> This one is broken by good intentions.

**Slide 6 — Rule 4**
> Flooring waits for paint.
> The most common sequencing failure in turnover.
> Protection, drips, and sometimes new flooring replaced.

**Slide 7 — Rule 5**
> The clean waits for all three.
> Repairs, paint, flooring, then clean.
> Break it and you buy a second clean.

**Slide 8 — Rule 6**
> QA waits for the clean.
> Photos taken before the final clean cannot be listed.
> That costs a day rather than a fee.

**Slide 9 — Rule 7**
> Relist waits for QA.

**Slide 10 — Close**

> Put the reason next to each rule.

## Script

### 0:00 to 0:20 — Cold open

**On screen:** the daily digest in Telegram, full frame. No slate, no logo, no face.

**Spoken:**

> This is every vacant unit a property manager has in turn right now, ranked. It arrives once a day. Six units, and the number at the top is the rent those six are losing while they sit.

### 0:20 to 0:50 — Reading the list

**On screen:** slow scroll down the six lines. Cursor rests on line 1, then on the line carrying the highest dollar figure.

**Spoken:**

> Top of the list is 12B. It has been at paint for four days, and paint is supposed to take two.
>
> The unit that has cost the most money is further down. It has been empty longer, but it is still inside the time its current stage should take.
>
> That is the whole ranking. Not what has cost the most, which you cannot change. What is stuck, which you still can.

### 0:50 to 1:45 — Beat 2, a full loop on 3 Oak

**On screen:** switch to the vendor phone view. QuickHaul sends a normal message, typed live, in ordinary words. Then cut to the manager Telegram.

**Spoken:**

> Here is the trash-out crew on 3 Oak, telling me they are done. This is a normal message. No form, no portal, nothing they had to install.

**On screen:** the approval card appears in the manager chat.

> That message was matched to the right unit and the right stage, the order was checked, and the stage closed. Then it drafted the next vendor's message and stopped.
>
> Repairs is next, so Dale gets a handoff. Nothing has gone to him yet.

**On screen:** press Email. Cut to the vendor mailbox showing the received message.

> I choose how it goes out. Email, message, or both. I picked email, and that is Dale receiving it.

**On screen:** cut to Airtable, the 3 Oak record, stages updated.

> And this is Airtable updating behind it. Not a dashboard I built. When this is installed, the company owns the base, not me.

### 1:45 to 2:25 — Beat 3, a second loop on 12B

**On screen:** Rivera sends a completion for 12B. Approval card appears.

**Spoken:**

> Same thing on 12B. The painter says he is finished, and the flooring handoff drafts.

**On screen:** press Both. Show the Telegram delivery and the email arriving.

> This time I send it both ways. One approval, two separate deliveries, tracked separately.

**On screen:** briefly show Statewide Flooring now holding two units at flooring.

> Worth noticing what just happened. Statewide Flooring now has two of my units sitting at the same stage.

### 2:25 to 2:50 — Beat 4, the ambiguity

**On screen:** Statewide replies to the 12B handoff message using the reply action, typing only "done".

**Spoken:**

> So when they say done, that word on its own is not an answer. It could be either unit.
>

**On screen:** Airtable showing 12B advanced and 7 Oak unchanged.

> 12B moves. 7 Oak does not. If there had been nothing to tell them apart, it would have asked me instead of guessing.

### 2:50 to 3:15 — Beat 5, the refusal

**On screen:** the refusal, with its reason visible.

**Spoken:**

> This one matters more than it looks. When something is marked done that the order does not allow yet, it refuses, and it says why.
>
> It refuses me too. I am the one paying for it, and it still says no. I can override that, and the override gets recorded.

**Capture note, read before filming.** Two ways to produce a refusal, and they are not equally verified:

- **Verified live:** press Done twice on the same unit. The second press is refused with a reason naming the stage as already complete. This has been observed in a real run.
- **Not verified on camera:** a vendor claiming a stage whose prerequisites are still open, for example a cleaner reporting 14A clean while repairs are still active. Expected behavior is that nothing moves and the manager is notified, which is a routing outcome rather than an ordering refusal.

**Rehearse whichever you choose and confirm the exact on-screen wording before recording.** Do not narrate a refusal you have not just watched happen.

### 3:15 to 3:35 — Beat 6, the silence

**On screen:** a stall alert in Telegram for a unit where nothing has arrived.

**Spoken:**

> Nothing happened on this one. No message, no update, nobody chased it.
>
> That is the alert. A vendor who finished and said nothing looks exactly like a vendor who never started, and both cost the same per day. So the silence is the thing worth telling you about.

### 3:35 to 4:00 — Beat 7, the limits

**On screen:** static. Either the Airtable base or a plain frame. No animation.

**Spoken, unhurried:**

> What this does not do.
>
> It does not write into your property management system. It does not choose which vendor gets the job. It does not tell the resident anything.
>
> And it will not message a vendor without someone tapping a button. Ignoring the card is a valid answer. Nothing goes out.

## Capture checklist

Run in this order. Several of these were learned the hard way.

1. **Reset immediately before recording.** Fixture ages are relative to the reset and drift in real time. Recording six hours later puts 12B at 104 hours instead of 98, and the narration stops matching the screen.
2. **Wait for the second Airtable reconciliation pass** before filming any Airtable frame. The first pass writes stages before the links exist, so linked records show empty. Roughly five minutes.
3. **Apply the Stages view sort in Airtable** (Turn, then Sequence) or film stages from inside a Turn record. Airtable preserves no useful default row order.
4. **Probe the tunnel** before filming anything involving Telegram. A dead quick tunnel looks healthy from the n8n side and silently swallows every button press.
5. **Clear the Telegram chats** of previous cards, digests and alerts.
6. **Fire a fresh digest** so the numbers on screen match the state you just reset to.
7. **Let the stall alerts fire once and delete them**, unless you are filming beat 6, in which case keep one.
8. **Rehearse beat 5** and confirm the exact refusal wording before recording it.
9. **State on screen that the data is synthetic.** One line is enough.

- The word "product" is never spoken. The framing is "something I built for a management company's turnover process."
- Beat 7 volunteers the limits before anyone asks, and is not rushed.
- The synthetic-data disclosure appears on screen and in the YouTube description.
- No client is referenced or implied. There is no client.
- Beat 5's capture note prevents narrating a refusal that has not been observed, per the verification rule in `AGENTS.md`.
